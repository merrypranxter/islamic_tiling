# Art Historical Context of Islamic Geometric Patterns

## 1. Origins: 8th–10th Century

### 1.1 The Abbasid Period (750–1258 CE)

Islamic geometric ornament emerged as a distinct artistic tradition during the **Abbasid
caliphate**, which ruled from Baghdad (founded 762 CE) and presided over the Islamic Golden Age
of science, philosophy, and art. The Abbasids drew heavily on the Hellenistic mathematical
tradition preserved and extended by Arab and Persian scholars.

**Key early examples:**
- **The Great Mosque of Córdoba** (*Mezquita*, 784–987 CE): The Andalusian Umayyad addition
  of geometric mosaic vaults in the *maqsura* (c. 965 CE) represents an early synthesis of
  Hellenistic geometric knowledge with Islamic decorative intent.
- **Samarra stucco panels** (c. 836–883 CE): The three "Samarra styles" include bevelled
  vine-scroll and early geometric interlace, the precursor to later star patterns.
- **Nilometer, Cairo** (861 CE): Early geometric carved stone borders.

### 1.2 Pre-Islamic Mathematical Sources

The geometric vocabulary had deep roots:
- **Greek geometry** (Euclid, Archimedes): the *Elements* provided the construction methods
- **Roman mosaic tradition**: geometric floor mosaics used many of the same star-polygon motifs
- **Sasanian Persian metalwork**: geometric interlace was already an established craft tradition
- **Byzantine church mosaics**: provided immediate visual models for early Islamic craftsmen

### 1.3 The Transformation

What distinguishes Islamic from its predecessors is not the individual motifs (stars, interlace)
but the **systematisation** — the development of constructive methods for generating endless
variations from a small set of principles, and the commitment to covering entire wall surfaces
(the concept of the **horror vacui** — filling every space).

## 2. Key Monuments

### 2.1 The Dome of the Rock, Jerusalem (691 CE)

The earliest surviving large-scale Islamic building contains mosaic decoration that is primarily
vegetal (arabesque) rather than geometric. It establishes the tradition of full-surface
decoration, but the geometric star-polygon tradition had not yet fully emerged.

### 2.2 The Alhambra, Granada (1238–1358 CE)

The palace complex of the Nasrid dynasty is the most famous expression of Islamic geometric art
in the Western world. Key features:

**Geometric patterns:**
- Tile dados (*alicatado*) in complex polychromatic geometric patterns
- Carved stucco (*yesería*) walls with intricate interlace
- Artesonado (coffered wooden ceilings) with star-polygon compositions

**Mathematically notable:**
- The Alhambra contains examples of **all 17 wallpaper groups** (a claim sometimes disputed;
  see section 4 below)
- The *Mexuar* hall tiles are among the earliest clear examples of p4g symmetry in Islamic art
- The Comares Hall ceiling uses a complex octagonal strapwork with 8-fold symmetry

**Specific rooms:**
- *Salón de Comares*: 11.3m square room with an 18m ceiling of cedar wood carved in
  interlocking octagonal stars — 8017 interlocking pieces
- *Sala de las Dos Hermanas*: muqarnas dome above geometric tile dado
- *Patio de los Arrayanes*: geometric tile floor complementing the reflecting pool

### 2.3 The Topkapi Scroll (c. 15th century CE)

A 29.5m × 0.33m accordion-folded scroll held at the **Topkapi Palace Museum**, Istanbul (MS H
1956). It contains 114 geometric panels and construction diagrams, documenting the craft
knowledge of geometric pattern-making.

Studied exhaustively by **Gülru Necipoğlu** (1995, *The Topkapi Scroll*). Key findings:
- The scroll documents both the **finished patterns** and the **construction sub-grids**
- It shows girih tiles used as modular design elements
- Provides evidence for a sophisticated systematic design tradition in the 15th–16th century
- Was likely a reference document for craftsmen, not a presentation piece for patrons

### 2.4 The Darb-i Imam, Isfahan (1453 CE)

See `darb_imam_analysis.md` for full analysis. Historically significant as:
- One of the most mathematically complex Islamic geometric patterns ever made
- Identified as quasicrystalline by Lu and Steinhardt (2007)
- Demonstrates the sophisticated design capability of Timurid-era craftsmen in Isfahan

### 2.5 The Sultan Hassan Mosque-Madrasa, Cairo (1356–1363 CE)

Commissioned by the Mamluk sultan al-Nasir Hassan, this massive structure (area 7,906 m²) is
notable for:
- Marble inlay strapwork in the courtyard (*sahn*) using 4-fold and 8-fold patterns
- Stone mosaic floor patterns with intricate geometric interlace
- The minaret (84m, tallest medieval minaret) decorated with geometric stonework

### 2.6 The Selimiye Mosque, Edirne (1569–1575 CE)

Designed by the architect Mimar Sinan at age 80 (by his own account), this Ottoman masterpiece
includes:
- Iznik tile revetments with 10-fold star patterns
- The transition from the Timurid/Mamluk geometric tradition to the Ottoman mode
- Complex muqarnas pendentives supporting the massive dome

## 3. Regional Variations

### 3.1 Persian (Iranian) Style

**Characteristics:**
- Preference for 5-fold and 10-fold (decagonal) symmetry
- Complex multi-level patterns (as at Darb-i Imam)
- Combination of geometric patterns with calligraphic bands and floral arabesque
- Extensive use of tilework (*kashi*): cut mosaic tile (*mu'arraq*) and painted tile (*haft-rang*)

**Key periods and centres:**
- Seljuk period (11th–12th c.): Khorasan and Isfahan
- Il-Khanid period (13th–14th c.): Maragha, Tabriz, Isfahan — innovative geometry
- Timurid period (15th c.): Samarkand, Herat, Isfahan — highest technical achievement
- Safavid period (16th–17th c.): Isfahan as imperial capital; the great mosques of the *Maidan*

### 3.2 Moroccan/Maghrebi Style

**Characteristics:**
- Strong use of **zellige** tile (hand-cut geometric tiles assembled from smashed tile blanks)
- Preference for 6-fold and 12-fold symmetry (simpler geometric proportions)
- Polychromatic colour: green, blue, yellow, white, black
- Integration with carved cedarwood (*zouak*) and carved plaster (*gebs*)

**Key examples:**
- **Medersa Bou Inania**, Fez (1350–1357 CE): zellige dado with elaborate geometric composition
- **Medersa Ben Youssef**, Marrakech (1564–1565 CE, rebuilt): star hexagonal zellige
- **Hassan II Mosque**, Casablanca (1993 CE): modern continuation of zellige tradition

### 3.3 Andalusian Style

**Characteristics:**
- Synthesis of Moroccan Berber, Persian, and Spanish Mudejar elements
- Alicatado tiles (coloured tile cut and assembled on site)
- Blend of geometric, vegetal, and calligraphic ornament

**Key examples:**
- **Alhambra** (see above)
- **Real Alcázar, Seville** (14th c. Mudejar, built after Reconquista)
- **Great Mosque of Córdoba** (8th–10th c.)

### 3.4 Central Asian (Timurid/Mughal) Style

**Characteristics:**
- Monumental scale with correspondingly large geometric patterns
- Combination of carved brick, cut tile, and painted tile (*haft-rang*)
- Complex polychromatic compositions (turquoise, cobalt, white, green, black, terracotta)
- 5-fold and 8-fold patterns common; sophisticated use of muqarnas

**Key examples:**
- **Gur-e-Amir mausoleum**, Samarkand (1403 CE): tile mosaic on the drum
- **Shah-i-Zinda necropolis**, Samarkand (14th–15th c.)
- **Masjid-i Shah (Imam Mosque)**, Isfahan (1611–1629 CE): Safavid masterwork of haft-rang tile

### 3.5 Ottoman Style

**Characteristics:**
- Iznik tilework with characteristic cobalt-blue on white ground (16th–17th c.)
- Transition from geometric emphasis to more floral/naturalistic Iznik style over time
- Polychrome geometric borders in muqarnas and carved stone

**Key examples:**
- **Rüstem Pasha Mosque**, Istanbul (1563 CE): considered finest Iznik tile collection
- **Selimiye Mosque**, Edirne (see above)
- **Topkapi Palace** tiled rooms (16th–17th c.)

## 4. The 17 Wallpaper Groups

### 4.1 What are Wallpaper Groups?

The **17 wallpaper groups** (or plane symmetry groups) are the complete classification of all
possible 2D repeating patterns by their symmetry. Each group is defined by its combination of:
- **Translations** (shifting the pattern)
- **Rotations** (2-fold, 3-fold, 4-fold, or 6-fold — the only crystallographic ones)
- **Reflections** (mirror symmetry)
- **Glide reflections** (translation + reflection combined)

These 17 groups were classified completely by the crystallographer Fedorov (1891) and by Schoenflies
(1891), and independently by Pólya (1924) who illustrated them with drawings.

### 4.2 Which Groups Appear in Islamic Art

Many Islamic geometric patterns utilise wallpaper groups systematically. The most common in
Islamic architecture:

| Group | Symmetry | Common in Islamic art | Example |
|-------|----------|----------------------|---------|
| p1 | Translation only | Rare | Simple offset grids |
| p2 | 2-fold rotation | Moderate | Some border patterns |
| pm | Mirror + translation | Common | Simple star borders |
| pg | Glide reflection | Moderate | Zigzag bands |
| cm | Mirror + glide | Common | Interlace borders |
| p2mm | Rectangle, full | Common | Octagonal tile layouts |
| p2mg | Mirror + rotation | Common | Hexagonal borders |
| p2gg | Double glide | Moderate | Diagonal grids |
| p4 | 4-fold rotation | Very common | Square-based stars |
| p4mm | Square, full | Very common | Octagonal patterns |
| p4gm | 4-fold + glide | Common | Pinwheel octagons |
| p3 | 3-fold rotation | Common | Hexagonal stars |
| p3m1 | 3-fold + mirror | Common | Hexagram patterns |
| p31m | 3-fold + mirror (alt) | Common | Triangle stars |
| p6 | 6-fold rotation | Very common | 12-pointed stars |
| p6mm | Hexagonal, full | Very common | 12-star on hexagonal lattice |
| p2mm | (see above) | | |

**Note on the Alhambra "17 groups" claim:**
The claim that the Alhambra contains all 17 wallpaper groups is a popular but contested
assertion. Different scholars count differently depending on how they categorise the actual
patterns. Makovicky (1992) confirmed at least 13 groups; other counts range from 13 to 17. The
claim that medieval Islamic craftsmen *deliberately* sought all 17 groups is certainly false —
they had no knowledge of the abstract classification — but they empirically explored a wide range.

### 4.3 Symmetry Groups NOT Available to 5-fold Patterns

Crucially, 5-fold rotational symmetry is **not** in the list of 17 wallpaper groups. This is the
crystallographic restriction theorem in action: no periodic pattern can have 5-fold rotation.

The aperiodic quasicrystalline patterns (like Darb-i Imam) have 10-fold *orientational* symmetry
but no *translational* symmetry — so they belong to **none** of the 17 wallpaper groups. They
require a different mathematical framework: the **quasicrystal symmetry groups**, which are
properly described by 4D or 5D lattice theory.

## 5. The Craftsmen: Transmission of Knowledge

### 5.1 The Craft Tradition

Islamic geometric patterns were produced by a specialised craft tradition, with distinct roles:
- **The muhandis** (مهندس, "engineer/geometer"): the master designer who planned the geometric
  composition and provided templates
- **The ustādh** (استاد, "master craftsman"): the senior craftsman who executed the work and
  trained apprentices
- **The apprentices**: who learned by doing, copying templates under supervision

### 5.2 Transmission Mechanisms

**Scrolls and pattern books:** The Topkapi Scroll is the most complete surviving example of what
must have been a widespread tradition of craftsman's notebooks containing geometric patterns,
construction instructions, and templates for specific motifs.

**The girih tile system itself** functioned as a knowledge-transmission tool: instead of requiring
each craftsman to understand the geometry from first principles, the tile system encoded the
geometry in physical form. A craftsman who could correctly assemble the tiles would automatically
produce correct geometric patterns.

**Workshop training:** Young men joined workshops at 10–14 years old and trained for 7–10 years
before being considered masters. Geometric patterns were learned by executing them repeatedly,
developing spatial intuition that preceded formal mathematical understanding.

**Guild organisation:** In major cities (Cairo, Isfahan, Istanbul, Fez), craftsmen were organised
into guilds (*asnaf* or *hirfa*) that regulated training, quality, and trade.

### 5.3 The Muhandis-Craftsman Relationship

The relationship between theoretical geometry (as studied by mathematicians) and craft practice
has been debated. Al-Buzjani's 10th-century treatise explicitly addresses the collaboration:

> "A group of geometers and artisans gathered together ... The artisans were skilled in practice
> but had no geometric theory, while the geometers had the theory but could not execute the work
> with their hands. As a result, each group needed what the other had."

This suggests a productive collaboration, with mathematicians providing theoretical frameworks
and craftsmen providing practical constraints and aesthetic judgment.

## 6. Modern Scholars

### 6.1 Keith Critchlow (1933–2020)

British artist, architect, and scholar. His 1976 book **Islamic Patterns: An Analytical and
Cosmological Approach** (Thames & Hudson) brought Islamic geometric patterns to wide Western
attention. Critchlow emphasised the cosmological and spiritual dimensions of the patterns —
their connection to Platonic geometry, Sufi thought, and the nature of number. While his
interpretations are sometimes considered speculative by art historians, the book remains a
landmark introduction.

### 6.2 Jay Bonner (b. 1952)

American artist and researcher. His monumental 2017 book **Islamic Geometric Patterns: Their
Historical Development and Traditional Methods of Construction** (Springer, 608pp) is the most
comprehensive single-volume technical study of the field. Bonner documents the polygonal
sub-grid method in exhaustive detail, with hundreds of construction diagrams. He also maintains
an active practice creating new Islamic geometric designs.

### 6.3 Gülru Necipoğlu (b. 1956)

Professor of Islamic Art and Architecture at Harvard University. Her 1995 book **The Topkapi
Scroll: Geometry and Ornament in Islamic Architecture** (Getty Center) is the definitive study
of the Topkapi scroll and of the relationship between mathematical theory and architectural
practice in the medieval Islamic world. She argues against purely cosmological interpretations
and for understanding patterns in their specific historical and architectural contexts.

### 6.4 Peter J. Lu and Paul J. Steinhardt

See `darb_imam_analysis.md` for full account of their 2007 *Science* paper. Lu's contribution
was identifying the girih tile system from photographic analysis of medieval monuments;
Steinhardt brought expertise in quasicrystal physics.

### 6.5 Peter R. Cromwell

British mathematician who has published extensively on Islamic geometric patterns and their
mathematical properties, particularly the relationship to Penrose tilings and quasi-periodicity.
Key papers include "The Search for Quasi-Periodicity in Islamic 5-fold Ornament" (2009) and
"Islamic Geometric Designs from the Topkapi Scroll: Unusual Arrangements of Stars" (2010).

### 6.6 Issam El-Said (1938–1988) and Ayşe Parman

Their 1976 book **Geometric Concepts in Islamic Art** (World of Islam Festival Publishing) was
influential in documenting construction methods and in presenting Islamic geometric art to an
architectural audience.

## 7. Living Tradition: Contemporary Islamic Geometric Artists

The tradition of Islamic geometric art is emphatically alive, practised today in multiple forms:

### 7.1 Traditional Craftsmen

**In Morocco:** zellige craftsmen (*maalem*) in Fez, Marrakech, and Casablanca continue to
produce hand-cut zellige using traditional methods. Major contemporary projects include the
Hassan II Mosque (1993) and numerous new hotels and government buildings.

**In Iran:** *kashi* tilework masters continue working in Isfahan, Shiraz, and Mashhad.
Contemporary religious architecture (mosques and shrines built since the 1979 Islamic Revolution)
has generated significant patronage for traditional geometric tile art.

**In Turkey:** geometric stonework and tile restoration specialists work on Ottoman monuments
under the supervision of the Ministry of Culture and Tourism.

### 7.2 Contemporary Artists Working in the Tradition

- **Eric Broug** (Netherlands/UK): Teaches Islamic geometric art through his Islamic Geometric
  Patterns studio; author of *Islamic Geometric Patterns* (Thames & Hudson, 2008) and multiple
  sequels. Active in online education.
- **Richard Henry** (UK): Produces intricate hand-drawn Islamic geometric compositions with
  complex multi-level symmetry.
- **Samira Mian** (UK): Creates Islamic geometric art and teaches workshops; focuses on making
  the tradition accessible to non-specialists.
- **Craig Kaplan** (Canada): Computer scientist who develops software for generating Islamic
  geometric patterns, combining mathematical rigour with artistic sensibility.

### 7.3 Digital Tools

Contemporary Islamic geometric art benefits from digital tools:
- **Vector drawing software** (Adobe Illustrator, Inkscape): precise construction of the
  underlying geometry
- **Custom software** (e.g., the code in this repository): algorithmic generation of patterns
  from mathematical specifications
- **3D printing and CNC routing**: realising designs in physical materials at architectural scale
- **Computer graphics shaders**: real-time rendering of strapwork with correct over-under
  weaving and lighting
