# Regional Styles in Islamic Geometric Art

## Overview

Islamic geometric patterns are not a single monolithic tradition — they are a family of
regional schools, each with distinct preferences for symmetry order, colour, material,
and compositional style. The same mathematical principles (girih tiles, pentagrid,
strapwork) manifest very differently in a Moroccan zellige panel, an Ottoman Iznik tile,
a Timurid Persian mosaic, and an Andalusian carved stucco.

Understanding regional distinctions is essential for:
1. Correctly identifying the provenance of historical works
2. Avoiding anachronistic "universal Islamic style" thinking in modern design
3. Choosing authentic colour palettes and symmetry orders for specific references

---

## 1. Andalusian Tradition (8th–15th Century CE)

### 1.1 Geographic Scope

The Iberian Peninsula under Islamic rule (*al-Andalus*): present-day Spain and Portugal.
Key cities: **Córdoba**, **Seville**, **Granada**, **Toledo**, **Almería**.
Period: Umayyad Emirate (711 CE) through the fall of Granada (1492 CE).

### 1.2 Dominant Symmetry Orders

**8-fold (octagonal)** is the definitive Andalusian symmetry. The {8/3} octagram appears
on virtually every architectural surface in the Alhambra. Second most common: 4-fold
(square) and 6-fold (hexagonal).

**10-fold (decagonal)** is RARE in Andalusian work — the quasiperiodic girih style
associated with Persian/Central Asian work did not transfer to al-Andalus in the same
depth. This is one of the clearest regional differentiators.

**12-fold** appears occasionally in elaborate carpet-page manuscript illumination from
Andalusian Qur'ans, but seldom in tile or stucco.

### 1.3 Materials and Technique

**Tile (alicatado):** Cut ceramic tiles (*azulejos*) assembled into geometric patterns.
The tiles are cut from larger fired and glazed pieces using a hammer (*martillo*) and
chisel. The Alhambra's tile dados are executed in this technique.

**Carved stucco (yesería):** Wet plaster (*yeso*) carved while still soft, producing
intricate three-dimensional strapwork and arabesque. The Alhambra's upper walls are
almost entirely carved stucco. This technique allows very fine geometry (gaps of a
few millimetres) impossible in cut tile.

**Woodwork (artesonado):** Interlocking geometric wooden ceilings, often using
complex 8-fold star patterns assembled from shaped wooden pieces without nails.
The Comares Hall ceiling (8017 pieces) is the supreme example.

### 1.4 Colour Palette

The Nasrid dynasty (Alhambra) palette:
- **Deep red** and **deep blue** as primary field colours (from the Nasrid flag colours)
- **White/ivory** for stucco and light tile
- **Gold** applied as thin gilded leaf on stucco
- **Turquoise/green** for secondary tile areas
- The palette tends toward high contrast: strong darks against bright lights

Characteristic: **polychrome tile dados** transition to **monochrome white stucco** higher
on the wall — a deliberate compositional choice that anchors the eye at ground level and
creates a sense of weightlessness above.

### 1.5 Compositional Conventions

- Patterns are typically **symmetrically arranged** within clearly bounded panels
- The concept of the **central medallion**: a single dominant rosette at the centre of
  a panel, surrounded by a symmetrical field
- Borders are important: thick geometric band borders frame panel compositions
- The characteristic Alhambra aesthetic: maximum density, minimum void ("horror vacui")
  alternating with large plain surfaces (plain white plaster), creating rhythm by contrast

## 2. Persian / Iranian Tradition (9th–17th Century CE)

### 2.1 Geographic Scope

The Persian cultural sphere: present-day Iran, Afghanistan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan,
Azerbaijan, parts of Iraq. Key cities: **Isfahan**, **Shiraz**, **Tabriz**,
**Samarkand**, **Bukhara**, **Herat**, **Yazd**.

### 2.2 Dominant Symmetry Orders

**10-fold (decagonal)** is the supreme Persian symmetry, reaching its highest
mathematical expression in the Darb-i Imam quasicrystalline portal (1453 CE,
Isfahan). The Persian tradition is responsible for the greatest developments in
5-fold/10-fold geometry.

**12-fold** is more common in Persian work than in Andalusian, especially in
Timurid-era (14th–15th century) designs. The 12-fold star appears in the main
fields of major portal compositions.

**8-fold** is present but secondary. Persian 8-fold work tends to be more complex
than Andalusian 8-fold — more tiers of subsidiary patterns, more levels of hierarchy.

### 2.3 Materials and Technique

**Mosaic faience (*qashani / kashi*):** Individual pieces of glazed tile cut to fit
the pattern, assembled in mortar. This technique allows the most complex multicolour
patterns and is the Persian equivalent of Moroccan zellige. Named after the city of
Kashan, a major tile-producing centre.

**Under-glaze painted tile (Iznik-style, in later Persian work):** Entire tiles
painted with geometric patterns before firing. Allows subtle colour gradients
impossible in cut tile.

**Brick geometry:** A distinctly Persian feature: the brickwork itself IS the
geometric pattern. In Seljuk towers (Gunbad-i Qabus, 1006 CE; Gunbad-i Kabud,
1197 CE), the bricks are cut and laid in patterns that create three-dimensional
relief geometry from a flat facade.

### 2.4 Colour Palette

The Persian palette:
- **Lapis lazuli blue** (*lazhward*): the dominant colour, associated with the sky
  and divine transcendence
- **Turquoise** (*firozeh*): the second great colour, associated with the colour
  of the desert sky
- **White** (*safid*): for strapwork lines and geometric outlines
- **Black** (*siyah*): as outline colour; also as a deep field colour in some schools
- **Saffron/gold** (*zard*): for accent and gilded lustre ware
- **Terracotta/rust** (*anar*): used in early Seljuk work; fades in Timurid period

Characteristic: **cobalt blue + turquoise** as the dominant colour pair — this is the
most distinctively Persian combination, and strongly associated with the domes and
portals of Isfahan.

### 2.5 Compositional Conventions

- Large **portal compositions** (*pishtaq*): the main facade of a mosque or shrine
  is treated as a single geometric canvas, with a single overriding composition
- **Spandrel fields**: the triangular areas beside arches are filled with geometric
  or floral patterns that complement but do not repeat the main field
- **Two-level compositions**: the same pattern at two scales (the Darb-i Imam
  technique) is a specifically Persian development
- **Medallion and field**: a central 10- or 12-fold star medallion in the centre
  of a field of repeating smaller patterns

## 3. Moroccan Tradition (11th Century CE — Present)

### 3.1 Geographic Scope

Morocco, Algeria, and Tunisia; also exported to sub-Saharan Africa and parts of
Iberian al-Andalus via cross-strait transmission. Key cities: **Fez** (*Fès*),
**Marrakesh**, **Rabat**, **Meknès**, **Tetouan**.

### 3.2 Dominant Symmetry Orders

**6-fold (hexagonal)** is uniquely prominent in Moroccan work — far more than in
Persian or Ottoman traditions. The {6/2} hexagram and {12/5} dodecagram appear
constantly in Moroccan zellige.

**8-fold** is common and takes a distinctly Moroccan form: the "khatam" (خاتم,
"seal") pattern, an 8-pointed star surrounded by a specific arrangement of squares
and rectangles.

**10-fold and 12-fold** appear in the most complex compositions but with a distinctly
Moroccan character — sharper contrasts, stronger colour use.

### 3.3 Materials and Technique

**Zellige (zillij, الزليج):** Small hand-cut ceramic tile pieces (tesserae), roughly
4cm × 4cm, cut from larger glazed tiles using a special hammer (*m'qacha*) and
then assembled face-down in a bed of mortar. The craftsman (*maâlem*) cuts each
piece by hand, guided by a template but without mechanical assistance. A single
zellige panel can require tens of thousands of individually cut pieces.

Zellige is unique in the Islamic world for its intensity of colour and the
complexity of the cut shapes. The assembly is done with the pattern facing down;
when the panel is flipped, the geometric face is revealed — the craftsman works
by feel and by memory.

**Carved plaster (*gebs*):** As in Andalusia, wet plaster is carved for wall
panels and capitals. Moroccan gebs tends to be *lower relief* than Andalusian
yesería, with thinner elements and denser compositions.

**Carved cedar woodwork (*charfia*):** Intricately carved wooden screens,
ceilings, and door panels. Moroccan woodwork is typically darker (natural
cedar colour) and uses different pattern conventions from Persian woodwork.

### 3.4 Colour Palette

The Moroccan zellige palette is the most polychrome in the Islamic world:
- **Emerald green** (*hadra*): uniquely Moroccan; no other regional tradition
  uses this shade of green as a primary field colour
- **Cobalt blue** (*zarqa*): universal, but a specific Moroccan shade (cooler
  and greener than Persian lapis blue)
- **Yellow/saffron** (*safra*): prominent as a background or secondary colour
- **Black** (*khal*): heavily used as grout and as a tile colour — the strong
  black grid of zellige is uniquely Moroccan
- **White** (*byada*): for strapwork elements
- **Terracotta/rust**: for warm-zone patterning

Characteristic: **green + blue + yellow + black** is the Moroccan signature.
No Persian or Andalusian work consistently uses this specific combination.

### 3.5 Compositional Conventions

- **Wainscot panels**: lower wall dado of zellige to about 1.5m height; above
  this, carved plaster panels; above that, carved wood cornice
- The three-material layered wall is unique to Morocco
- **Fountain compositions**: central courtyard fountains have zellige base
  panels as their signature decorative element (the Attarine Medersa, the
  Bou Inania Medersa, Fez)
- **Radial symmetry**: Moroccan compositions favour strongly radial (N-fold
  symmetric about a centre) designs, with the rosette centre explicitly
  celebrated as the design's focal point

## 4. Ottoman Tradition (13th–20th Century CE)

### 4.1 Geographic Scope

The Ottoman Empire at its height: Anatolia, the Balkans, the Levant, Egypt,
North Africa. Key cities: **Istanbul** (*Constantinople*), **Edirne**, **Bursa**,
**Iznik** (the tile-producing centre), **Cairo**, **Jerusalem**, **Baghdad**.

### 4.2 Dominant Symmetry Orders

**8-fold** is the dominant Ottoman symmetry — the Ottomans inherited the Seljuk
8-fold tradition and brought it to its industrial peak.

**6-fold** is prominent in Ottoman manuscript illumination (*tezhip*) where
hexagonal compositions are common in border patterns.

**10-fold** is present but treated differently from Persian usage: the Ottomans
tended to use 10-fold as a single large central motif rather than as an infinite
field pattern.

### 4.3 Materials and Technique

**Iznik Tiles (16th–17th Century):** The most technically advanced ceramic
tradition in the Islamic world. Iznik pottery produces:
- Perfect white slip ground (achieved through a special kaolinite formula)
- The unique **"Armenian bole" red** (introduced c. 1530 CE): a thick, raised
  tomato-red pigment unlike any other Islamic red, applied last and fired
  separately
- Under-glaze painting, not cut tile — allowing curved, painted geometric
  lines rather than cut geometric shapes

The major monuments decorated with Iznik tile:
- **Rüstem Pasha Mosque, Istanbul** (1561 CE, Mimar Sinan): floor-to-ceiling
  Iznik tile, densest concentration in the world
- **Selimiye Mosque, Edirne** (1569–75 CE): panels of 10-fold geometric work
  in Iznik tile alongside floral compositions
- **Topkapi Palace, Istanbul** (15th–19th centuries): successive campaigns
  of Iznik tile in various rooms

### 4.4 Colour Palette

The Ottoman Iznik palette:
- **Brilliant white** (*beyaz*): the Iznik trademark — no other tradition achieves
  this pure, opaque white ground
- **Cobalt blue** (*lacivert*): a slightly darker, richer blue than Persian
- **Turquoise** (*turkuaz*): lighter and more "liquid" than Persian turquoise
- **Armenian bole red** (*kırmızı*): unique to Iznik; slightly orange-toned
- **Emerald green** (*yeşil*): not as dominant as in Morocco
- **Black outline**: thin black painted outlines separate all colour fields

Characteristic: **white + cobalt + bole red** is the quintessential Ottoman palette.
No other regional tradition has this specific red.

### 4.5 Compositional Conventions

- **Integration of geometric and floral**: the Ottomans uniquely combine geometric
  patterns with hyper-naturalistic floral motifs (*rumi*, *hatayi*) in the same
  composition — a development that Persian and Moroccan traditions keep more separate
- **Large-scale geometric fields** surrounded by floral borders
- **"Saz" style**: long curved leaves interweaving with geometric frames
  (specific to the royal Ottoman workshops, *nakkaşhane*, from mid-16th century)
- **Manuscript illumination (*tezhip*)**: Ottoman tezhip is among the world's most
  refined — the geometric framework is subordinated to the floral overall impression

## 5. Mamluk Tradition (1250–1517 CE)

### 5.1 Geographic Scope

Egypt and Syria under the Mamluk sultans. Key city: **Cairo**.

### 5.2 Distinctive Features

The Mamluk tradition represents a distinct synthesis of Persian geometric depth
and Egyptian boldness:
- **Polychrome marble inlay** (*opus sectile*): cut marble panels for floor and
  wall applications, far more durable than ceramic tile
- Heavy use of **4-fold and 8-fold** patterns in a specific Cairo style
- **"Ablaq" polychrome stonework**: alternating light and dark stone courses,
  creating geometric striped patterns on the exterior of buildings
- **Bronze-inlaid metalwork** with dense geometric patterns: lamp, candlestick,
  and basin surfaces covered with complex 8-fold and 10-fold strapwork

The Sultan Hassan Mosque-Madrasa (1356–63 CE, Cairo) is the supreme Mamluk example.

## 6. Central Asian Tradition (9th–16th Century CE)

### 6.1 Geographic Scope

Transoxiana: present-day Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, and surrounding regions.
Key cities: **Samarkand**, **Bukhara**, **Khiva**, **Merv**.

### 6.2 Distinctive Features

- **Timurid polychrome tilework** (15th century): The most spectacular use of
  colour in the entire Islamic world, with exterior dome surfaces entirely clad
  in blue and turquoise mosaic tile
- **Large-scale compositions**: Timurid buildings are enormous; patterns are
  designed to be legible from great distance, with bold geometry
- **The two-level girih hierarchy** (as at Darb-i Imam) is a specifically
  Central Asian/Persian development
- **Terracotta carved brick** (earlier Samanid-Karakhanid period): geometric
  patterns cut in brick achieve the maximum effect with the minimum material

The **Registan** complex in Samarkand (Ulugh Beg Madrassa, 1420 CE;
Shir-Dor Madrassa, 1636 CE; Tilla-Kori Madrassa, 1646 CE) represents the
summit of Central Asian polychrome architecture.

## 7. Comparative Summary Table

| Feature | Andalusian | Persian | Moroccan | Ottoman | Mamluk |
|---------|-----------|---------|----------|---------|--------|
| Primary symmetry | 8-fold | 10-fold | 6-fold, 8-fold | 8-fold | 8-fold |
| Secondary symmetry | 4-fold | 12-fold | 12-fold | 6-fold | 4-fold |
| Key material | Carved stucco, cut tile | Mosaic faience | Zellige | Iznik tile | Marble inlay |
| Dominant colours | Red + blue + gold | Blue + turquoise | Green + blue + yellow | White + cobalt + red | Cream + black + gold |
